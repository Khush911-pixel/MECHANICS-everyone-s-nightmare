Lesson 03 · Dynamics

Forces and Newton’s laws

Kinematics describes how an object moves. Dynamics asks how interactions change that motion. Newton’s laws connect the two, one carefully chosen object at a time.

Beginner to intermediate · About 15 minutes · Builds on vectors and acceleration

1. What is a force?

A force is a push or pull arising from an interaction between objects. A hand pushes a book; Earth pulls it downward; a table pushes upward where it touches the book.

Force is a vector. Its SI unit is the newton (N), where 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². Because force has direction, add forces as vectors before finding their combined effect.

Start with one object. Name the object whose motion you want to explain. Then list the forces acting on it, each supplied by another object or field.

2. Newton’s first law

If the net external force on an object is zero, its velocity stays constant. It remains at rest or continues in a straight line at steady speed.

ΣF = 0 ⟹ a = 0Zero net force means no change in velocity

Continued motion does not require a continuing net force. A net force is needed to change velocity, including to change direction.

Example · A book on a table

Earth pulls the book down; the table pushes it up. These forces balance on the book, so its acceleration is zero.

3. Newton’s second law

The net force determines acceleration. For constant mass, acceleration points in the direction of the net force.

ΣF = maNet force (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration (m/s²)

Σ means “add all the forces.” Add components along each axis, then apply the equation to the net force.

Worked example · Pulling a crate

A 4 kg crate is pulled with 18 N while friction opposes with 6 N. The net force is 12 N, so a = 12/4 = 3 m/s² in the pull direction. Units: N/kg = m/s².

4. Newton’s third law

When object A exerts a force on object B, B simultaneously exerts an equal magnitude force in the opposite direction on A. These are an interaction pair.

Force of A on B = − force of B on AThe pair acts on two different objects

The forces do not cancel on a diagram of just one object. A swimmer pushes water backward; the water pushes the swimmer forward.

5. Free body diagrams

A free body diagram isolates one object and shows every external force acting on it. Draw the object simply, then draw and label each force arrow.

  1. Choose and isolate one object.
  2. Identify every object or field interacting with it.
  3. Draw one force for each interaction and label its source.
  4. Choose axes and resolve angled forces into components if helpful.
  5. Apply ΣF = ma along each axis.
Velocity is not a force. An object moving right does not automatically have a force to the right. Forces describe interactions; velocity describes motion.

6. Common forces

  • Weight: Earth’s gravitational force near its surface, approximately W = mg downward, with g ≈ 9.8 m/s².
  • Normal force: a contact force perpendicular to a surface. It is not always equal to weight.
  • Tension: a pull transmitted along a taut string or cable.
  • Friction: a contact force that resists relative slipping or its tendency. A simple kinetic model is fₖ = μₖN.

Here μₖ is dimensionless and N is the normal force magnitude. Static friction adjusts to the situation up to a maximum; it does not always equal μₛN.

7. Equilibrium

Translational equilibrium means acceleration is zero, so the vector sum of forces is zero. The object may be at rest or moving at constant velocity.

ΣFₓ = maₓ   ·   ΣFᵧ = maᵧApply the second law separately along perpendicular axes

Quick check

1. What net force is needed for steady straight-line motion?

Zero net force. With zero acceleration, velocity stays constant.

2. A 2 kg object has a net force of 10 N east. Find its acceleration.

a = F/m = 10/2 = 5 m/s² east.

3. You push on a wall. Where does the equal and opposite force act?

The wall pushes on you. The two forces act on different objects.
