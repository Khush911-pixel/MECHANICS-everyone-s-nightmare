Lesson 04 · Conservation

Work, energy, and momentum

Forces can change motion in ways that are easier to track through energy or momentum. These ideas let us solve many problems without following every instant of an object’s path.

Beginner to intermediate · About 16 minutes · Builds on Newton’s laws

1. Work: force through a displacement

In physics, a force does work when it has a component along an object’s displacement. For a constant force, work is the dot product of force and displacement.

W = Fd cos θF is force magnitude, d is displacement magnitude, θ is the angle between them · unit: joule (J)

A push in the direction of motion does positive work. A force opposite the motion does negative work. A perpendicular force does zero work, even if it changes the direction of travel.

For a changing force along a line, the work is the signed area under the force-versus-position graph.

Example · Pulling a box

A 20 N pull acts 3 m along the direction of motion. The work done by the pull is W = (20)(3) = 60 J. If the same pull were perpendicular to the displacement, its work would be zero.

2. Kinetic energy and the work-energy theorem

Kinetic energy is the energy associated with motion. It depends on an object’s mass and speed, and it cannot be negative.

K = ½mv²    and    Wnet = ΔKThe net work on an object equals its change in kinetic energy

Positive net work increases speed; negative net work decreases it. The work-energy theorem is Newton’s second law viewed across an interval rather than moment by moment.

Worked example · Braking

A 1000 kg car slows from 20 m/s to 10 m/s. Its kinetic energy changes by ΔK = ½(1000)(10² − 20²) = −150,000 J. The net work on the car is −150 kJ: braking removes kinetic energy.

3. Potential energy and conservation

Some interactions let us define potential energy, stored according to the arrangement or position of objects. Near Earth’s surface, gravitational potential energy can be written Ug = mgh, where h is measured from a chosen reference height. For an ideal spring, Us = ½kx².

Only changes in potential energy matter in these models; the zero level can be chosen for convenience. When only conservative forces do work, kinetic and potential energy trade while their sum remains constant.

Emech = K + U = constantApplies when nonconservative work, such as friction, is absent or negligible

With friction or another nonconservative force, mechanical energy can change. The energy does not disappear: it may transfer to thermal energy, sound, deformation, or other forms.

Choose the right system. If Earth and the object are both in your system, gravity can be represented by potential energy. If only the object is in the system, gravity appears as an external force doing work.

4. Power

Power measures how quickly work is done or energy is transferred.

P = W / ΔtAverage power · unit: watt (W), equal to one joule per second

Two motors can transfer the same amount of energy but have different power if one does it in less time. For an object moving under a force, instantaneous power is P = F · v.

5. Momentum and impulse

Momentum describes motion with both mass and velocity. It is a vector, so direction matters.

p = mv    and    J = ΔpImpulse J equals the change in momentum · units: kg·m/s or N·s

A net force acting over time changes momentum: for constant net force, J = FnetΔt. A smaller force applied for longer can produce the same momentum change as a larger force applied briefly.

This is why airbags and padding help in a collision: increasing the stopping time can reduce the average force for the same change in momentum.

Example · Catching a ball

A 0.15 kg ball moves toward a catcher at 20 m/s and is brought to rest. Its momentum changes from 3 kg·m/s to zero. The catcher’s force on the ball gives an impulse of −3 N·s, opposite the ball’s initial motion.

6. Conservation of momentum

For a system with no net external impulse, total momentum stays constant. During a brief collision, forces between the colliding objects can be large, but they are internal to the system and come in equal-and-opposite pairs.

pbefore = pafterFor an isolated system; add momentum as a vector

In a perfectly inelastic collision, objects stick together. Kinetic energy usually decreases, while total momentum is conserved. In an elastic collision, both momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved.

Worked example · Two carts stick together

A 2 kg cart moving at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg cart, and they lock together. Initial momentum is (2)(3) + (1)(0) = 6 kg·m/s. Their shared final velocity is 6/(2 + 1) = 2 m/s.

Quick check

1. A force acts perpendicular to an object’s displacement. How much work does it do?

Zero, because cos 90° = 0.

2. If the net work on an object is negative, what happens to its kinetic energy?

It decreases over that interval.

3. Two carts collide and stick together. Which quantity is conserved if external forces are negligible?

Total momentum is conserved. Kinetic energy generally is not conserved in a perfectly inelastic collision.
