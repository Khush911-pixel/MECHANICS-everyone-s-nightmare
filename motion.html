Lesson 02 · Kinematics

Describing motion

Kinematics is the description of motion: where an object is, how its position changes, and how that change itself evolves. We can begin without asking what caused the motion.

Beginner · About 12 minutes · Builds on position and vectors

1. Position and displacement

Position tells where an object is relative to a chosen origin. In one dimension, a coordinate such as x = 4 m places it four metres along the positive direction from that origin.

Displacement compares a final position with an initial one. It is a signed change in position, so it keeps track of direction.

Δx = xfinal − xinitialDisplacement = final coordinate minus initial coordinate

If you walk from x = 2 m to x = 9 m, your displacement is +7 m. If you then return to x = 5 m, the displacement on the return trip is −4 m. Your total distance for the whole walk is 11 m, while your net displacement is +3 m.

Keep these separate: distance is the length of the path taken; displacement is the net change in position.

2. Average and instantaneous velocity

Average velocity compares displacement with the elapsed time. Its sign indicates direction along the chosen axis.

vavg = Δx / ΔtUnits: metres per second (m/s)

Instantaneous velocity describes the motion at a particular moment. On a position-versus-time graph, it is the slope of the curve at that moment. A steep slope means a large speed; a negative slope means motion toward decreasing x.

Speed is the magnitude of velocity, so speed is never negative. For a journey that changes direction, average speed and the magnitude of average velocity can differ.

3. Acceleration

Acceleration measures how velocity changes over time. A velocity can change because an object speeds up, slows down, or turns.

aavg = Δv / ΔtUnits: metres per second squared (m/s²)

Choose positive and negative directions before interpreting signs. If east is positive, a negative velocity means the object moves west. A negative acceleration means velocity is changing toward the west; it does not always mean the object is slowing down.

Example · Slowing while moving east

A cart moves east at 8 m/s and, 2 seconds later, moves east at 2 m/s. With east positive, its average acceleration is (2 − 8) / 2 = −3 m/s². The cart is still moving east, but its eastward velocity is decreasing.

4. Constant acceleration

When acceleration stays constant, a small set of equations connects position, velocity, and time. They are useful models for motion over an interval where the acceleration does not change much.

v = v₀ + at
Δx = v₀t + ½at²Constant acceleration; v₀ is initial velocity and t is elapsed time

These equations describe the motion once the starting conditions are known. They do not tell us what physical force produced the acceleration; that question belongs to dynamics and Newton’s laws.

Worked example · A cart gains speed

A cart starts with v₀ = 2 m/s and accelerates at 1 m/s² for 4 s.

  • Final velocity: v = 2 + (1)(4) = 6 m/s.
  • Displacement: Δx = (2)(4) + ½(1)(4²) = 16 m.

Check the units: m/s + (m/s²)(s) gives m/s, and (m/s)(s) plus (m/s²)(s²) gives metres.

5. Read motion graphs

A graph is another way to see the relationships:

  • The slope of a position-time graph gives velocity.
  • The slope of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration.
  • The signed area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement.

For constant acceleration, position changes along a curve while velocity changes along a straight line. Try adjusting the model below and compare its values at different times.

Quick check

1. A person walks 5 m east, then 5 m west. What is the net displacement?

Zero metres: the final position is the same as the starting position.

2. On a position-time graph, what does a horizontal line mean?

Position is not changing, so velocity is zero during that interval.

3. An object moves west and has acceleration west. Is it speeding up or slowing down?

It is speeding up: velocity and acceleration point in the same direction.
