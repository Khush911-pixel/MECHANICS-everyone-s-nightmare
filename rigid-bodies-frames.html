Rigid bodies and non-inertial frames

A rigid body can rotate in three dimensions, and an accelerating observer can describe motion in a frame that is not inertial. Both topics extend Newton’s laws while making the reference frame and geometry explicit.

Advanced · About 20 minutes · Builds on rotation and Newton’s laws

1. What makes a body rigid?

An ideal rigid body keeps the distance between every pair of its points fixed. Real objects deform a little, but the rigid-body model works well when those deformations do not strongly affect the motion.

A body's motion can be split into translation of its centre of mass and rotation about that centre. For pure rotation, each point shares the same angular velocity vector, though its linear velocity depends on its position: v = ω × r.

v(point) = vCM + ω × rr is measured from the centre of mass in this rigid-body relation

2. Moment of inertia in three dimensions

For rotation about one fixed axis, a single moment of inertia I can be enough. In general three-dimensional rotation, mass can be distributed differently in different directions. This is captured by the inertia tensor I, which relates angular velocity to angular momentum.

L = I · ω    ·    Krot = ½ ω · LAngular momentum need not point in the same direction as angular velocity

The tensor is symmetric and can be diagonalized by choosing special directions called principal axes. Along those axes, the relation simplifies to L₁ = I₁ω₁, L₂ = I₂ω₂, L₃ = I₃ω₃.

For a body with one fixed point and body axes aligned with principal axes, Euler’s equations connect applied torque with changes in its angular velocity components:

τ₁ = I₁ω̇₁ + (I₃ − I₂)ω₂ω₃
τ₂ = I₂ω̇₂ + (I₁ − I₃)ω₃ω₁
τ₃ = I₃ω̇₃ + (I₂ − I₁)ω₁ω₂Components are measured along the body's principal axes

The coupled terms explain why a freely spinning asymmetric object can have surprising motion. Rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable: a small disturbance can make the body tumble.

3. Gyroscopes and precession

A rapidly spinning wheel has angular momentum along its spin axis. When an external torque acts, it changes the direction of angular momentum. The wheel can respond by turning sideways rather than simply tipping in the direction of the push.

τ = dL/dtTorque changes angular momentum; it need not point along L

For a simple top with a rapidly spinning rotor, the gravitational torque can produce slow precession of the spin axis. The detailed motion depends on the top's geometry, spin rate, and initial conditions.

Use vector directions. In three dimensions, scalar “clockwise” rules can hide important signs. The cross product direction in τ = r × F or τ = Ω × L keeps the geometry explicit.

4. Inertial and non-inertial frames

An inertial frame is one in which Newton’s first law holds: an isolated object keeps constant velocity. A frame that accelerates or rotates relative to an inertial frame is non-inertial.

In an accelerating frame, the same physical motion can still be described, but the equation of motion includes extra terms. For a frame with origin acceleration a₀ and angular velocity Ω, the relative acceleration arel obeys:

m arel = Freal − m a₀ − 2mΩ × vrel − m Ω̇ × r − m Ω × (Ω × r)Translational, Coriolis, Euler, and centrifugal terms, respectively

These extra terms are often called inertial forces. They arise from describing motion in the accelerating frame. Their effects are measurable within that frame.

5. The rotating-frame terms

  • Translation term: −ma₀ appears if the frame’s origin accelerates in a straight line. In an elevator accelerating upward, a person feels heavier because the floor must accelerate them upward.
  • Centrifugal term: −mΩ × (Ω × r) points outward from the rotation axis for steady rotation. It accounts for the outward tendency seen in the rotating frame.
  • Coriolis term: −2mΩ × vrel acts on an object moving relative to the rotating frame. Its direction is perpendicular to both Ω and the relative velocity.
  • Euler term: −mΩ̇ × r appears when the frame’s rotation rate changes.

For a fixed rotation axis and constant rotation rate, Ω̇ = 0 and the Euler term vanishes. If the object is at rest relative to that frame, the Coriolis term also vanishes.

6. Choosing a frame

Use an inertial frame when Newton’s laws are easiest to apply there. Use a non-inertial frame when it simplifies the geometry or matches what an observer measures, and include the corresponding inertial terms.

For example, the ground is often approximated as inertial for a short laboratory experiment. For large-scale motion over Earth’s surface, Earth’s rotation can matter and Coriolis effects may be important.

Good habit: State which frame you are using before writing equations. Then distinguish real interactions from terms introduced by the frame.

Quick check

1. Why can angular momentum and angular velocity point in different directions for a rigid body?

The inertia tensor can map angular velocity to angular momentum in a different direction when the mass distribution is not symmetric about the spin direction.

2. When does the Coriolis term vanish in a rotating frame?

It vanishes when the object has no velocity relative to that frame, or when the relative velocity is parallel to the rotation axis.

3. Is centrifugal force an interaction with another object?

No. It is an inertial term included when describing motion in a rotating frame.
