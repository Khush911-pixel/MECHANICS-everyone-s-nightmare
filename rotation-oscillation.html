Lesson 05 · Rotation & oscillation

Rotation and oscillation

A wheel turns, a door swings, and a spring vibrates. These motions reuse familiar ideas—position, velocity, acceleration, force, and energy—with angular quantities and repeating patterns.

Intermediate · About 18 minutes · Builds on forces, energy, and momentum

1. Angular position and motion

To describe rotation about a fixed axis, track an angle θ. Angles in equations are measured in radians: one full revolution is 2π radians. Angular displacement Δθ describes how far and in which sense an object turns.

Angular velocity ω is the rate of change of angle, and angular acceleration α is the rate of change of angular velocity. For constant angular acceleration, the familiar constant-acceleration equations apply with x, v, and a replaced by θ, ω, and α.

ω = dθ/dt    ·    α = dω/dt
ω = ω₀ + αtAngular velocity: rad/s · angular acceleration: rad/s²

For a point a distance r from the axis, tangential speed is v = rω. Points farther from the axis travel faster even though every point on a rigid wheel shares the same angular velocity.

2. Torque: the turning effect of force

A force can turn an object when its line of action does not pass through the chosen axis. The torque magnitude is τ = rF sin θ, where r points from the axis to the force application point and θ is the angle between r and F.

τ = rF sin θ    ·    Στ = IαTorque unit: newton metre (N·m) · fixed-axis rotation, constant moment of inertia

The perpendicular distance from the axis to the force’s line of action is the moment arm. A larger moment arm creates more torque for the same force. This is why a long wrench helps loosen a tight bolt.

For rotation about a fixed axis, moment of inertia I measures resistance to angular acceleration. It depends on both how much mass there is and how that mass is distributed relative to the axis.

Example · Opening a door

Pushing near the handle gives a larger moment arm than pushing near the hinges. The same perpendicular push therefore creates greater torque and angular acceleration.

3. Rotational inertia and energy

For a collection of point masses rotating about an axis, I = Σmr². Moving mass farther from the axis increases I strongly because distance is squared. Extended objects have standard expressions for I, found by adding up their mass distribution.

Krot = ½Iω²Rotational kinetic energy about a fixed axis

A rolling object may have both translational and rotational kinetic energy. For rolling without slipping, the point touching the ground is instantaneously at rest relative to the ground, and vcm = Rω.

Ktotal = ½Mvcm² + ½Icmω²Translation of the centre of mass plus rotation about it

4. Angular momentum

Angular momentum is the rotational counterpart of linear momentum. For a rigid body spinning about a fixed principal axis, L = Iω. An external net torque changes angular momentum.

Στext = dL/dtIf net external torque is zero, angular momentum is conserved

A figure skater who pulls in their arms reduces their moment of inertia. With little external torque, angular momentum stays nearly constant, so their angular speed increases.

Conservation depends on the system. Check whether external torque acts about the axis you chose before using angular momentum conservation.

5. Static equilibrium

A rigid body in static equilibrium has no linear acceleration and no angular acceleration. Both the net force and the net torque must be zero.

ΣF = 0    and    Στ = 0Choose a pivot that makes unknown torques easier to remove

For a balanced plank, clockwise and counterclockwise torques about a pivot must cancel. The same object can be in translational equilibrium yet begin rotating if the net torque is not zero.

6. Simple harmonic motion

Oscillation is motion that repeats around an equilibrium position. In simple harmonic motion, the restoring force is proportional to displacement and points back toward equilibrium. An ideal spring obeys Hooke’s law, F = −kx.

F = −kx    ·    ω = √(k/m)    ·    T = 2π√(m/k)Ideal mass-spring oscillator · k in N/m · T is the period

At the equilibrium point, speed is greatest and spring potential energy is smallest. At the turning points, speed is zero and spring potential energy is greatest. In the ideal model, energy continually trades between kinetic and potential forms.

For a simple pendulum at small angles, the period is approximately T = 2π√(ℓ/g). The small-angle condition matters; large swings do not have exactly the same period.

Quick check

1. Why does a longer wrench help turn a bolt?

It increases the moment arm, giving more torque for the same perpendicular force.

2. If a body’s moment of inertia halves while angular momentum stays fixed, what happens to angular speed?

It doubles, because L = Iω remains constant.

3. What happens to a spring oscillator’s period if its mass increases while the spring stays the same?

The period increases according to T = 2π√(m/k).
