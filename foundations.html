Lesson 01 · First principles

The language of motion

Before describing how an object moves, we need a clear way to measure, locate, and compare things. This lesson builds that shared language: units, coordinates, and vectors.

Beginner · About 10 minutes · No calculus required

What mechanics asks

Mechanics studies how physical objects move and what changes that motion. A useful description starts by deciding what object we are tracking, where we measure its position from, and how we measure time.

Those choices form a model. For a first pass, we might treat a rolling ball as a point whose location changes over time. That leaves out its size and spin, but can still answer a question about where it will be a moment later.

Working habit: say what object you are studying and what your measurements mean before writing equations.

1. Give every measurement a unit

A measurement is a number paired with a unit. “The table is 2” is incomplete; “the table is 2 metres long” tells us what was measured and on what scale.

Mechanics often uses three basic dimensions: mass M, length L, and time T. Units are standards for measuring them: kilogram, metre, and second in the SI system.

QuantityCommon SI unitDimension
Position or distancemetre (m)L
Timesecond (s)T
Masskilogram (kg)M
Speedmetre per second (m/s)L/T
Accelerationmetre per second squared (m/s²)L/T²

Dimensions describe the kind of quantity; units tell us the measurement scale. Metres and kilometres are different units for length, but both have dimension L.

A quick equation check

If speed is distance divided by elapsed time, its units must be metres divided by seconds: m/s. If a calculation for speed ends in seconds alone, something went wrong. Dimensional checks can expose impossible equations, though matching units cannot prove an equation is correct.

2. Choose a coordinate system

Position has meaning relative to a reference. On a straight track, mark a zero point and call rightward positions positive. In a room, use perpendicular directions on the floor and a third vertical direction.

The origin and directions are choices. A good choice makes the question simpler, but changing coordinates does not change the physical motion.

Position = (x, y)For a point on a flat surface, relative to a chosen origin and axes

A marker at (3 m, 2 m) is 3 metres along the first axis and 2 metres along the second. The ordered pair keeps both components of its position.

3. Scalars and vectors

A scalar has size only: time, mass, and energy are examples. A vector also has a direction: position change, velocity, acceleration, and force are vectors.

In two dimensions, a vector can be described by its components. A displacement of 3 metres east and 2 metres north has components (3 m, 2 m). Components are signed measurements along the chosen axes.

When components are perpendicular, use a right triangle to find the vector’s length: √(x² + y²). The component ratio gives its direction.

Worked example · A short walk

You walk 3 m east, then 4 m north. Your distance travelled is 7 m. Your displacement is the straight-line vector from start to finish:

components = (3 m east, 4 m north)
magnitude = √(3² + 4²) m = 5 m

The direction is about 53° north of east. Distance describes the route; displacement describes the net change in position.

Vector addition: add matching components. The 3 m east and 4 m north displacements combine to (3 m, 4 m), not to a 7 m displacement in one direction.

Why do we add components?

Each component measures change along its own axis. Adding horizontal parts gives the total horizontal change; adding vertical parts gives the total vertical change. The resulting pair describes the combined vector.

Quick check

1. Is “12 seconds” a scalar or a vector?

A scalar: it has a magnitude and unit but no direction.

2. A runner goes 6 m east, then 8 m west. What are distance and displacement?

Distance is 14 m. Taking east as positive, displacement is −2 m: 2 m west.

3. Can dimensional analysis prove a formula is right?

No. It can show a formula is inconsistent when units do not match, but matching dimensions alone do not establish the physical relationship.
