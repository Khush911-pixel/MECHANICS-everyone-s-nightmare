Central forces and advanced mechanics

The same physical motion can be described with forces, energy, or more compact mathematical frameworks. Central-force motion reveals conserved quantities; Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics make those ideas systematic.

Advanced overview · About 20 minutes · Builds on the full learning path

1. Central forces

A central force always points toward or away from a fixed centre, and its magnitude depends only on distance r from that centre. Gravity from a spherical body outside it is a familiar example.

F(r) = F(r) r̂    and    τ = r × F = 0The force is parallel to the radius, so its torque about the centre is zero

With zero torque about the centre, angular momentum is conserved. The motion stays in a plane perpendicular to the conserved angular-momentum vector. This reduces a three-dimensional problem to motion in one plane.

If the force comes from a potential U(r), the radial motion can be understood using an effective potential: the real potential plus a centrifugal term associated with angular momentum.

Ueff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2mr²)For a particle of mass m with angular-momentum magnitude L

2. Gravity and orbits

Newtonian gravity between masses M and m has magnitude GMm/r² and points toward M. A circular orbit occurs when gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration.

vcircular = √(GM/r)    ·    vescape = √(2GM/r)r is distance from the attracting body's centre · G is the gravitational constant

For an ideal inverse-square gravitational force, bound trajectories are ellipses, with a circle as a special case. An object with enough energy to escape follows an unbound path. The orbit depends on both energy and angular momentum.

Example · A satellite farther out

A circular orbit farther from a planet has a lower orbital speed: vcircular scales as 1/√r. But its orbital period is longer because it also travels around a larger path.

3. Lagrangian mechanics

Instead of listing force components in ordinary coordinates, Lagrangian mechanics starts with a function called the Lagrangian. For many classical systems, it is kinetic energy minus potential energy: ℒ = T − U.

d/dt (∂ℒ/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂ℒ/∂qᵢ = 0Euler–Lagrange equation · one equation for each independent generalized coordinate qᵢ

A generalized coordinate can be an angle, a length, or another variable that describes the system’s configuration. Choose coordinates that already respect constraints, and the equations may become much simpler.

Example · A simple pendulum

For a pendulum of length ℓ and bob mass m, use its angle θ as the coordinate. The energies are T = ½mℓ²θ̇² and U = mgℓ(1 − cos θ). Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation gives θ̈ + (g/ℓ)sin θ = 0. At small angles, sin θ ≈ θ, recovering simple harmonic motion.

The method is especially useful for linked bodies, constrained motion, and systems where Cartesian force bookkeeping becomes awkward.

4. Symmetry and conserved quantities

A symmetry is a change in description that leaves the physics unchanged. In Lagrangian mechanics, continuous symmetries are tied to conserved quantities:

  • If the laws do not depend on absolute time, energy is conserved.
  • If the laws do not depend on absolute position, linear momentum is conserved.
  • If the laws do not depend on orientation, angular momentum is conserved.

This connection is a powerful guide: recognizing a symmetry can reveal the quantity that stays constant, even before solving the motion.

5. Hamiltonian mechanics

Hamiltonian mechanics describes a system using generalized positions q and their conjugate momenta p. The Hamiltonian H is built from the Lagrangian by a transform. For many ordinary conservative systems, it equals total energy.

q̇ᵢ = ∂H/∂pᵢ    ·    ṗᵢ = −∂H/∂qᵢHamilton’s equations evolve positions and momenta through phase space

Phase space is the space of all possible positions and momenta. A system’s state traces a path through it as time passes. This viewpoint is central in statistical mechanics, nonlinear dynamics, and many advanced treatments of classical physics.

Keep the roles clear. The Hamiltonian often represents energy, but that identification depends on the system and coordinates. The defining equations use H as the generator of time evolution.

6. Where classical mechanics fits

Classical mechanics is an excellent model when objects are much larger than atomic scales, speeds are well below the speed of light, and quantum effects can be neglected. It remains useful across engineering, astronomy, and everyday motion.

Relativity changes the description at very high speeds or in strong gravitational settings. Quantum mechanics is needed when wave-like and discrete effects matter. In their appropriate limits, both theories recover classical behavior for many familiar systems.

Advanced mechanics is not a collection of unrelated tricks. Newtonian, Lagrangian, and Hamiltonian descriptions organize the same physical predictions in different ways, each suited to particular questions.

Quick check

1. Why is angular momentum conserved for an ideal central force?

The force points along the radius, so torque about the centre is zero.

2. What is the Lagrangian for many basic conservative systems?

ℒ = T − U, kinetic energy minus potential energy.

3. Name one symmetry and its associated conserved quantity.

Examples: time-translation symmetry gives energy conservation; spatial-translation symmetry gives linear momentum conservation; rotational symmetry gives angular momentum conservation.
